# Async and remote-team communication tips

The same four habits, applied to messages, meetings, and follow-ups. Compiled from the free Communication Style Quiz tips and notes — not a new remote-work framework, and not a claim about what “high-performing teams” do.

[Take the free quiz](https://commstylequiz.com/?utm_source=github&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=a_repo&utm_content=async_tips) if you want a snapshot of the habit you reach for in everyday work moments (a 60-minute invite, unclear feedback, a group chat that will not decide).

## Before you hit send

- If the other person wants **Clarity**, lead with the request, then offer detail. Put the point and the “by when” first.
- If you want Clarity, add one beat of context or appreciation before the ask: “I know this landed suddenly. Here's what I need by Thursday.”
- If the other person wants **Connection**, reflect back one thing you heard before you solve it. A neat plan offered too early can feel like a brush-off, even when it was meant kindly.
- If you want Connection, still name your own view in the first half of the message, not only at the end. A kind but clear “no” is allowed: “I can't take this on this week.”
- If the other person wants **Structure**, give details and a little time. Specific examples help more than general feelings. “Which part, exactly?” is a fair question.
- If you want Structure, lead with the conclusion: “Short version: yes. Here's why...” In a fast thread, it is fine to say “Let me come back to you on that by 3 pm.” Share a rough draft earlier than feels comfortable.
- If the other person wants **Spark**, leave a little space for “what else could we try?” before you lock the plan. Build on their idea before you poke holes in it. Talk it through live if the thread is getting long — they would often rather call than type three long messages.
- If you want Spark, cut an important draft by a third and keep the single key point. Pause after sharing an idea and invite one reaction before you add the next.

## Meetings and group threads

Habits differ. None of these is the “right” meeting:

- Some people secretly want it to end in 25 minutes with three clear decisions.
- Some relax when someone who never speaks up shares a point.
- Some want an agenda — and for people to actually follow it.
- Some hope it goes off-script into an idea better than the plan.

Useful moves from the public tips, whoever you are:

- Start by saying what the conversation is for: a decision, a listen, a plan, or ideas.
- In a stalled discussion, it can help to ask “So what are we deciding?”
- When a group chat has been circling, post one message that lists the options and who wants what.
- Pin the time, the link or the address, and who's bringing what.
- At the end of a call, a three-bullet recap is often worth more than another ten minutes of talk.
- End a brainstorm by writing down one owner and one date for the best idea.

## When the plan changes, or the message is unclear

- Decide whether the next step is a new plan, a check on who this is hardest on, a list of what else it breaks, or a chance to improvise — people reach for different first moves.
- If feedback is vague (“it's good, but something's off”), try: “Got it. What's the one thing to fix first?” or “Which part, exactly? Can you show me an example?”
- Preferring to reply well tomorrow rather than badly right now is a valid Structure instinct. Say when you will come back, so the pause is not read as disagreement.
- Under stress, Clarity can skip the “why,” Connection can go along to keep the peace, Structure can go quiet, and Spark can jump between ideas. Notice which one you drop to.

## A note on shifting styles

Most people talk differently to a boss, a best friend and a group chat. The site describes adjusting your style to the other person as “communication accommodation.” Treat a quiz result as a default for this week, not a fixed label for a teammate.
